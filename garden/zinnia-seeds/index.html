Seed saving · Zinnias · Zinnia elegans

How to harvest
zinnia seeds.

Every petal on a zinnia has one seed attached to its base, shaped like a tiny arrowhead. Pull a single petal and you'll know in two seconds whether the whole head is ready.

Glassboro, NJ · Zone 7a Covers: ripeness test, sorting, drying, storage, color

Step zero

The one-petal test

You do not need to guess and you do not need to cut anything to find out. Walk up to the head and pull one petal.

The one thing to do

Pull a single petal off a dried head. If it releases easily and a hard brown arrowhead comes with it, the head is ready. If the petal tears and the base is soft and pale, wait.

Ready to cut

  • The whole head is brown, dry and crunchy. It rustles when you touch it.
  • The stem for a few inches below the head has gone brown and woody too.
  • A petal pulls away clean with almost no resistance.
  • There's a hard, dark brown arrowhead attached to the base of that petal.

Not yet

  • Any color left in the petals, even a faded pink.
  • The head still feels soft, spongy, or damp in the middle.
  • The stem below the head is still green.
  • The petal tears instead of releasing.
  • The bit at the base is white, pale green, or flat. That's an unfinished seed.

The decision

You have to let some flowers die

This is the trade nobody mentions, and it's worth deciding on purpose rather than by accident.

Deadheading is what keeps zinnias blooming. Cut a spent flower and the plant sends up more. Stop cutting and it decides its job is done and starts winding down.

Seed only forms on flowers you leave alone. So saving seed costs you blooms. There's no way around it.

The way to pay the least is to pick your plants deliberately.

  • Choose three to five plants, not the whole patch.
  • Pick the best ones. Strongest stems, the colors you actually want back, the fullest flower form.
  • Tie a strip of ribbon or a bit of flagging tape on each one so you don't deadhead it by reflex.
  • Keep deadheading everything else. Those plants carry the display through to frost.

Start tagging around six weeks before your first frost. That gives the heads time to go fully brown on the plant, which is the only place they properly finish.

If heavy rain is coming and the heads aren't finished, cut them with long stems and hang them upside down somewhere dry and airy. They'll finish indoors. The seed is a little weaker than plant-ripened seed, and it beats losing the lot to rot.

The job

Cut, crumble, sort, dry

Fifteen minutes of work spread over a few weeks. The sorting is the only part with any skill in it.

  1. Cut on a dry day

    Mid-morning, after the dew has gone, with no rain in the last day. Damp seed is the one thing that ruins this.

    Snip each head off into a paper bag or a bowl. Hold the bowl underneath as you cut, because dry heads shed seed on the way down.

    One bag per color or per variety if you're keeping them separate. Write on the bag now, not later.

  2. Crumble the head apart

    Hold a head over a bowl and crush it between your fingers. It falls apart into petals, seeds and dry brown bits.

    Work the base of the head too. The center florets carry seed as well as the outer petals do.

    Do the whole batch before you start sorting. It's faster in two passes than one.

  3. Sort the arrowheads out

    This is covered in its own section below, because it's the part that decides whether next spring works.

    Short version: keep the hard brown arrowheads, bin the thin papery slivers.

  4. Dry them properly, then dry them longer

    Spread the sorted seed one layer deep on a plate, a tray, or a paper towel. Out of direct sun. Room temperature.

    Stir it with a finger once a day so nothing sits damp underneath.

    Give it three weeks. They feel dry after three days. They are not dry after three days, and seed that goes into a jar early goes moldy in it.

  5. Label and store

    Paper envelope, then a sealed glass jar. Somewhere cool, dark and dry. A fridge is the best spot in most houses.

    An envelope of silica gel in the jar alongside pulls out whatever moisture is left.

    On the envelope: variety, color, the date, and the plant it came from. Nobody remembers in March.

The skill

Telling good seed from chaff

A crumbled zinnia head gives you far more rubbish than seed. Learn the shape once and you'll sort a bowl in a minute.

Keep these. Hard, dark brown or tan. Shaped like an arrowhead or a little spade, flat, with a point at one end and a wider shoulder at the other. Some have a papery wing on the shoulder. That's normal, keep them. They feel solid between finger and thumb.

Bin these. Thin, pale, papery slivers with no thickness. Anything white or greenish. Anything that folds instead of snapping. Broken petals, dust, and the dry brown scales from the head.

You'll get plenty of borderline ones. Keep them. Zinnias are generous and a bowl of seed will sow far more ground than you have.

If you want it properly clean for long storage, winnow it. Pour the seed slowly from one bowl into another with a fan on low, outdoors. Chaff blows sideways, seed drops straight down. For a home garden it's optional.

How long it keeps

Plan on three years, stored cool and dry. Some people get five. Germination fades gradually.

Test before you commit a bed. Roll ten seeds in a damp paper towel, seal it in a sandwich bag, leave it somewhere warm. Count sprouts at seven to ten days. Seven out of ten is a good packet.

The catch

You will not get the same flower back

Or rather, you'll get zinnias, and they'll be beautiful, and they will not match what you saved from. Here's why.

Zinnias can pollinate themselves, but bees cross them constantly. A patch of mixed colors is one big genetic blender. Seed from the coral one carries whatever the bee was carrying that morning.

To keep a color true, you'd need several hundred feet between varieties, or one variety per season, or a mesh bag over the blooms you're saving from. That's a real project, not a weekend job.

There's a second effect with the big double zinnias. Doubleness is carried across several genes, so it splits up in the next generation. Expect a share of your saved seed to come back as singles or semi-doubles even from a fully double parent.

None of that is a reason not to save. It's a reason to save from the best plant in the patch rather than a random one, and to enjoy what turns up.

If you want an exact color and an exact form next year, buy that packet. Save seed for volume, for the surprise, and for the plants that clearly liked your garden.
