Seed saving · Bee balm · Monarda

How to harvest
bee balm seeds.

A spent bee balm head is a crown of little dry tubes, and each tube is holding loose seed the way a cup holds sand. Tip it and you lose it. That one fact changes how you test, how you cut, and how you carry it inside.

Glassboro, NJ · Zone 7a Covers: ripeness test, the timing race, shaking, sifting, storage, cultivars

Step zero

The palm test

You are not looking for a head that has fully dried on the plant. With bee balm, fully dried on the plant usually means empty.

The one thing to do

Cup your open hand under a brown head and tap the stem once. If a few dark specks drop into your palm, the seed is ripe and it is already leaving. Cut that plant today.

Cut now

  • The flower petals have all dropped and the head is a bare brown or tan crown of tubes.
  • The head is dry and papery to the touch, no green left in it.
  • The stem for a few inches below the head has gone brown too.
  • A single tap over your hand drops a few dark specks.
  • Roughly two to four weeks past the last bloom on that stem.

Not yet

  • Any petals still hanging on, even shrivelled red ones.
  • The head still has green in the tubes, or feels soft and fleshy.
  • The stem below the head is still green.
  • A tap gives you nothing, or gives you pale slivers instead of dark specks.
  • Split a tube with a fingernail and the contents are white or hollow.
Bee balm is a mint. Look at the square stem and the smell on your fingers. What you are collecting is not really a seed, it is a nutlet, four of them tucked in the base of every little tubular flower. That is why they are so small, and why there is so much husk around so little seed.

The decision

This one is a race, not a wait

Every other seed guide here tells you to be patient. This one tells you the opposite, and it is worth understanding why before you plan your week.

A zinnia holds its seed until you crumble the head. A sunflower holds its seed until you rub it off. Bee balm holds nothing. The tubes are open at the top and the nutlets sit loose inside with room to move.

So the seed leaves on its own. A thunderstorm empties a patch. A goldfinch working the heads empties it faster. Tip a cut stem sideways on the walk back to the house and you will pour most of it into the lawn.

Red bee balm is the worst offender. Its tubes are roomier than the other species, so it sheds earliest and hardest. Wild bergamot and spotted bee balm hold on noticeably longer and are far more forgiving to a first-timer.

What this means in practice:

  • Start checking heads about two weeks after the last flower on that stem finishes.
  • Check every two or three days once they brown, not once a week.
  • Cut ahead of a forecast storm rather than after it. Slightly early beats empty.
  • Take the bag or bowl into the garden with you. Not to the edge of the garden. With you.

Red bee balm

Monarda didyma

The scarlet one people plant for hummingbirds. Sheds the fastest of the lot, so it wants the tightest watch and an early cut. Spreads hard by underground runners, which means you rarely need seed to get more of it.

Wild bergamot

Monarda fistulosa

The lavender native one. Holds its seed longer, gives more of it, and is genuinely easy. If you are learning this job, learn it on this plant.

Leave some standing anyway. Goldfinches eat these all autumn and the hollow stems are winter shelter for small native bees. Take seed from a few plants and let the rest of the patch go through the winter uncut. You do not need much. One head holds more seed than a whole bed needs.

The job

Cut upright, dry, shake, sift

The whole thing is ten minutes of hands-on work with a week of waiting in the middle. The only skill is not spilling it.

  1. Cut on a dry day, and cut into the bag

    Mid-morning, after the dew has burned off, with no rain in the last day. Damp seed is the one thing that ruins this.

    Hold the stem upright in one hand, snip four to six inches below the head, and lower the whole thing head-first into a paper bag without ever tilting it. Every degree you tip it costs you seed.

    One bag per species or per color if you are keeping them apart. Write on the bag now, not later.

  2. Leave the bag open for a week

    Fold the top loosely or leave it open. Somewhere dry and airy, out of the sun. A shelf in the garage is fine, a sealed tub is not.

    About a week. The heads finish drying and the tubes open up, which is what lets the seed come out cleanly instead of staying jammed.

    Do not skip to shaking on day one. Half of it stays stuck in the head.

  3. Shake it out

    Close the top of the bag and shake it hard for ten or fifteen seconds. Most of the seed comes free and collects in the corners.

    For the stubborn ones, hold a head over a deep bowl or a bucket and knock the stem against the rim. Then roll the head between your palms over the bowl to break the last of it loose.

    Bin the stems. What is left in the bowl is seed and a lot of crumbled husk.

  4. Sift with a kitchen strainer

    A fine mesh tea strainer or a small kitchen sieve does this in under a minute, and it is the reason bee balm is easier than it sounds.

    Tip the mix in and tap the side. The seed is small enough to fall through onto a plate. The husk and the broken tubes stay behind.

    If your mesh is too fine and nothing falls through, go up a size. If everything falls through, go down one.

  5. Dry it longer than feels necessary

    Spread the sifted seed one layer deep on a plate or a paper towel. Out of direct sun, room temperature.

    Stir it with a finger once a day so nothing sits damp underneath.

    Give it two to three weeks. It feels dry on day three. It is not dry on day three, and seed that goes into a jar early goes mouldy in it.

  6. Label and store

    Paper envelope, then a sealed glass jar, somewhere cool, dark and dry. A fridge is the best spot in most houses.

    A sachet of silica gel in the jar pulls out whatever moisture is left.

    On the envelope: species, color, the date, and the plant it came from. Nobody remembers in March.

The skill

Telling good seed from chaff

This is the hardest sort in the garden, because bee balm seed is about the size of a grain of sand and the chaff looks a lot like it.

Keep these. Tiny, hard, oblong or teardrop-shaped, dark brown to almost black, sometimes tan. They have weight for their size and a faint shine. Roll a pinch on white paper and the good ones roll. They will not look like a seed you have ever bought.

Bin these. Pale flat flakes. Anything that crumbles under a fingernail. The dry brown tube walls, which come out in bits the same length as the seed and are the main thing you are trying to lose. Anything hollow.

Tip the sifted pile onto a white plate and tilt it. Seed rolls to the low edge. Chaff drags and sticks. That one move separates most of it without a fan.

You do not need it perfectly clean. A little chaff in the envelope is harmless if the seed is genuinely dry. Chaff in a damp envelope is what grows mould, so the drying matters more than the cleaning does.

How long it keeps

Plan on two to three years stored cool and dry, and treat the first spring as the good one. Bee balm is not a seed that ages gracefully.

Test before you commit a bed. Roll ten seeds in a damp paper towel, seal it in a sandwich bag, leave it somewhere warm and light. Count sprouts at seven to fourteen days.

Sowing it next spring

Bee balm seed needs light to germinate, so press it onto the surface of moist soil and do not bury it. A dusting of fine vermiculite is as much cover as it wants.

Whether it needs a cold spell first depends on who you ask. Prairie Moon lists both red bee balm and wild bergamot as needing no pretreatment at all. Plenty of growers still cold-stratify out of habit and get fine results. The easy way out of the argument is to scatter it outdoors in late autumn and let winter decide.

The catch

Your named variety is not coming back

If the tag said Jacob Cline or Raspberry Wine or Pardon My Purple, read this part before you plan the bed.

Named bee balm varieties are propagated by cuttings and division, not by seed. Every plant sold under that name is a clone of one original. Seed from it is a fresh genetic roll, so the offspring are half a mystery.

Bee balms also cross with each other freely. Red bee balm and wild bergamot growing in the same yard will produce seedlings somewhere between the two, in flower color, in height and in how badly they get powdery mildew.

That last one is the part that actually costs you. Mildew resistance is the reason most modern varieties exist, and it is not reliably passed on. Saved seed from a clean, resistant plant can come back as a plant that goes grey by August.

None of this is a reason not to save. It is a reason to be honest about what you are saving for.

  • Want the same plant? Divide the clump in spring instead. Bee balm spreads by runners and divides easily, and a division is genuinely identical.
  • Want more plants, cheap? Save the seed. You will get bee balm, the pollinators will not care, and some of the seedlings will be better than the parent.
  • Want a true native for a meadow? Save from wild bergamot, and save from a stand with no fancy varieties nearby.
